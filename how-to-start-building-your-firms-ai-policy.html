How to Start Building Your Firm's AI Policy

A guide for solo and small-firm attorneys, based on DC Bar Ethics Opinion 388 and ABA Formal Opinion 512.

Written by Irene Rhodes (Chair, Young Lawyers Section) and Philippe Lin (Secretary, Young Lawyers Section)

01

What This Guide Is — and Isn't

This resource is not a model AI policy. It explains the decisions a firm AI policy needs to make, and the professional responsibility duties that inform them.

This guide is not
×A model AI policy you can copy and paste
×A clause bank or engagement letter addendum
×A vendor evaluation form
×A jurisdiction-specific determination of what your firm is ethically required to do

AI didn't create new professional duties. It changed how existing ones apply. Competence, confidentiality, supervision, candor, and reasonable fees are the same obligations they've always been. What's different is what those obligations require when the tool doing the work can make up citations, send client information to places it shouldn't go, and produce confident-sounding analysis that's just wrong.

02

Why This Matters Now

The consequences of ignoring this are no longer hypothetical. In DC specifically, the stakes changed in 2025.

$5K–$109K+
Documented sanctions for AI-generated filings containing fabricated citations
17–33%
Hallucination rate found by Stanford RegLab, even in paid, citation-aware legal research tools
May 6, 2025
DC Court of Appeals Order M284-24 added binding technology-competence language to Rule 1.1 and expanded Rule 1.6 to cover unauthorized access to client information, not just disclosure

Those dimensions are now enforceable rules, not advisory guidance. But a written policy does not, on its own, make a firm compliant.

“Adopting a policy is not a safe harbor, and a document alone does not mitigate, remove, or prevent mistakes. Only careful implementation, training, supervision, and verification can reduce those risks.” New Jersey Supreme Court, Notice to the Bar (March 2026)

What a policy does is give a firm a documented, defensible way to meet duties that already exist. The eight questions that follow are the decisions that policy needs to make.

03

The Risk Model

Not every AI use carries the same risk. Before working through the eight questions, it helps to have a mental model for how risk shifts with the use case.

"Controls should follow the task, not the tool."

Lower exposureHigher exposure

Internal administrative

No client data · No external output

Brainstorming, scheduling, internal drafts, summaries of public material

Client data involved

Confidentiality duties · Vendor vetting required

Reviewing client documents, drafting with case details, research with facts

Substantive legal analysis

Verification mandatory · Professional judgment

Legal research, case analysis, strategy memos, contract review

Client-facing or filed output

Candor obligations · Court disclosure rules

Court filings, client advice letters, intake chatbots, opposing counsel

Diligence, verification, and oversight increase with the consequences of error.

This is a teaching concept, not a formal legal classification. But it reflects what both Opinion 388 and Opinion 512 are built on: the more that's at stake if something goes wrong, the more diligence and verification you need. California's May 2026 updated guidance says the same thing.

04

The Eight Decisions

These are the decisions a first firm AI policy needs to make: what's legally required, and where the firm has genuine latitude to choose its own approach.

12345678
Decision No. 1

What AI use does your firm need to govern?

"AI" is broader than a chatbot. It includes general-purpose tools like ChatGPT or Claude, legal-specific research platforms with AI features, AI capabilities embedded in software you already use (document drafting assistants, email composition tools, AI-powered search in your practice management system), and potentially client-facing systems like intake chatbots or automated document assembly.

A policy limited to standalone chatbots will miss the AI already built into tools your firm adopted before anyone used the word "governance." The opposite failure is just as common and more dangerous: banning AI entirely without offering approved alternatives. When firms prohibit AI use without providing vetted options, staff use consumer tools on personal devices anyway, and the firm has no idea where client data is going. A blanket ban doesn't work as governance. It just pushes the risk somewhere you can't see it.

What your firm needs to decide

What tools, features, and use cases fall within your policy's scope? Does it cover embedded AI in existing software? Personal devices? Administrative uses with no client data? Client-facing systems?

Decision No. 2

Which tools will the firm permit, and how will you evaluate new ones?

Under Rules 5.1 and 5.3, the supervisory obligation to vet tools before they're used with client information is a hard ethical requirement. At minimum, DC Opinion 388 requires that before adopting any tool, a lawyer determine: whether inputs are visible to the provider or third parties, whether interactions could influence outputs shown to later users, what the vendor's data retention and training policies are, and what security and breach notification procedures exist.

The most common failure here is treating the brand name as the risk assessment. Saying "we allow Copilot" is not a confidentiality analysis. Those answers can differ between the free and paid tiers of the same product, and between default settings and enterprise configuration.

Required

A threshold assessment of any tool's data practices before client information is entered.

DC Rule 1.6, amended 2025 · ABA 512 vendor diligence framework

Governance Choice

Whether you maintain a formal approved-tools list, how you document the vetting process, who can approve a new tool, and how often approvals are reviewed.

Decision No. 3

What client information can go into an AI tool, and under what conditions?

This should be the longest section in any firm policy, because it's where the most duties overlap. For self-learning tools capable of exposing client information to others, ABA Opinion 512 requires informed client consent before the information is entered. That consent cannot be a generic engagement-letter clause.

One thing firms miss: assuming "deidentified" automatically means safe. Opinion 388 warns that combinations of seemingly non-identifying facts may permit reidentification. A related failure is skipping the terms of service. The difference between a tool's free tier and its paid enterprise tier is often the difference between "trains on your inputs" and "does not." That distinction is the entire confidentiality analysis.

Required

Assessment of data retention and training practices before inputting client information; informed consent (specific to the tool and the risk) before using a self-learning tool with client data.

DC Rules 1.1 and 1.6, amended 2025 · ABA 512

Governance Choice

What categories of information the firm permits in which tools, whether the firm prohibits client data in consumer-tier products entirely, and how consent is documented.

Decision No. 4

What human review does AI output require before it leaves the firm?

Professional judgment remains with the lawyer. Both DC 388 and ABA 512 reject blind reliance on AI output. For court filings, the floor is unambiguous: all citations, factual claims, legal analysis, and arguments must be independently verified against primary sources before submission.

This is the single most sanctioned AI failure in the legal profession. No tool is exempt. The Stanford RegLab study found that even paid, citation-aware legal research tools hallucinate on 17 to 33 percent of queries. And courts have been harsher on lawyers who discovered the error and didn't fix it than on the original mistake itself, treating concealment as a separate violation.

Required

Independent verification of AI-assisted factual and legal claims before submission to a court or delivery to a client. Compliance with any applicable court AI-disclosure standing orders.

DC Rules 1.1 and 3.3, amended 2025 · Rules 3.1, 8.4(c) · ABA 512

Governance Choice

What the minimum verification steps are by task type, whether higher-risk tasks (filings, client advice) require a second reviewer, and how review is documented.

Decision No. 5

When does the client need to know?

Client disclosure is not a blanket obligation, but it is required in at least five situations: the client asks whether AI was used; engagement terms or outside counsel guidelines require it; informed consent is needed because client information will enter the tool; AI use is relevant to the fee basis; or AI output will influence a significant decision in the representation.

The common mistake goes both ways: some lawyers assume AI use never requires disclosure, while others draft a generic "we use AI" clause and treat it as blanket consent. ABA 512 rejects boilerplate as sufficient informed consent when real consent is required, but it also rejects the other extreme, that every routine AI use needs client notification. A client may also prohibit or condition AI use through engagement terms, outside counsel guidelines, or a court's AI-specific standing order — these matter-specific restrictions override internal approval.

Required

Disclosure or informed consent when any of the five triggering circumstances applies. Compliance with client-imposed and court-imposed AI restrictions.

DC Rule 1.4 · ABA 512

Governance Choice

Whether the firm proactively discusses AI use in every engagement, how client instructions are recorded, and how the firm tracks court standing orders by judge.

Decision No. 6

Who is accountable for AI use by staff and contractors?

Rules 5.1 and 5.3 are not suggestions. Managerial lawyers must establish policies governing AI use, and supervisory lawyers must take reasonable steps to ensure compliance. Courts have sanctioned supervising attorneys personally for AI-generated content that made it into a filing under their name. The supervisory duty doesn't transfer risk downward. A partner who signs a brief is responsible for the citations in it, regardless of whether an associate, a paralegal, or an AI tool generated them.

A policy that covers lawyers but forgets staff is incomplete. Secretaries, paralegals, law clerks, contractors, and vendors all fall within the supervision obligation.

Required

Policies communicated, training provided, supervisory responsibility maintained regardless of who actually used the tool.

DC Rules 5.1, 5.3 · ABA 512

Governance Choice

Who conducts training, how often, whether specific tools require separate instruction, and how the firm documents that training occurred.

Decision No. 7

How does AI use affect billing and client records?

On billing, both opinions are direct: a lawyer bills for actual time spent, not for the time the task would have taken without AI. AI tool costs may be passed to the client only with the client's agreement and at actual cost, and lawyers may not bill for time spent learning to use a tool they'll use regularly, since maintaining technological competence is the lawyer's own obligation. Virginia's LEO 1901 diverges from ABA 512 here. A firm policy can't simply adopt one jurisdiction's billing rule as universal; it must reflect the fee rules governing your practice.

On client records, DC Opinion 388 raises a dimension most other opinions skip: lawyers should consider whether particular AI interactions connected with a matter belong in the client's file. That doesn't mean save every prompt. It's a judgment call about what counts as part of the representation record.

Required

Billing reflects actual time (hourly) or demonstrable reasonableness (flat fee); AI costs disclosed if passed through; no billing for learning time.

DC Rule 1.5 · ABA 512

Governance Choice

How AI tool costs are classified (overhead vs. pass-through), how fixed-fee arrangements account for AI efficiency, whether prompts or outputs are retained as part of the client file, and how AI-generated work product is labeled in firm systems.

Decision No. 8

How will the firm keep its governance current?

An AI policy doesn't end at adoption. Tools change their terms of service, vendors update data practices, bar guidance evolves, and courts issue new AI-specific standing orders. The competence and supervisory duties that require a policy in the first place also require keeping it current.

Two items worth adding to a "what to do next" list beyond the policy itself: first, check your professional liability insurance — policies written before widespread AI adoption may not cover AI-related errors, and carriers increasingly ask whether firms maintain documented AI policies and vendor diligence records. Second, build the habit of checking applicable court standing orders before any filing in which AI was used; the number of judges with AI-specific disclosure requirements continues to grow.

Required

Ongoing competence in the tools the firm uses; supervisory duties continue as tools and guidance evolve.

DC Rule 1.1, amended 2025 · ABA 512

Governance Choice

Review cadence, who owns the policy, incident reporting procedures, how changes in vendor terms trigger reassessment, and how the firm tracks new bar guidance and court orders.
